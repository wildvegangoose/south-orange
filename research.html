South Orange Public Library

The library's Local History wiki holds a historical timeline, street-name histories, the Village Annuals of 1891–94, photo and postcard galleries, and pages on Village Hall, the Connett library, Cameron Field and more. It also has a real-estate file of about 3,500 scanned listings for South Orange houses from 1960 to 2000, arranged by street. Its guide "Researching Your Historic House" is the best place to start.

Historical & Preservation Society and Seton Hall

The South Orange Historical & Preservation Society (founded 1986) champions the Old Stone House and local history. Seton Hall University Libraries hold 277 digitized South Orange postcards from the 1890s to the 1950s, plus yearbooks, The Setonian and the college scrapbook. High-resolution copies are available on request.

The Village's Historic Preservation Commission (2012) keeps the landmark list and can tell you whether your house is a designated property.

Step by step

How to research your South Orange house

Built on the South Orange Public Library's guide and the sources our research turned up.

  1. Estimate the date

    Start with Essex County tax records. The recorded construction date is a clue, not a fact, and is unreliable for older houses. About 85% of South Orange's houses date from the late 1800s to the 1930s.

  2. Find your block & lot

    Your tax-map block and lot number unlock deeds, permits and the historic-district inventories. The Old Stone House, for example, is Block 1007, Lot 13.

  3. Walk through the directories

    The Oranges city directories list South Orange households with the town code "S O". The Maplewood Memorial Library's digital archive has 33 volumes from 1892 to 1970. Look up your address year by year, and watch for renumbering: the Old Stone House has been 209, 219 and 201 South Orange Avenue.

  4. Read the maps

    The 1860 county map names the farm owners. Lewis P. Taylor's May 1873 "Map of South Orange & Montrose" shows Vose's lots. The Library of Congress has the Sanborn fire-insurance maps of 1912 and 1912–1949 online, free to reuse. Our Explore map layers USGS survey maps from 1888 onward.

  5. Check the historic districts

    If you live in Montrose Park, the 1997 National Register nomination describes the district and its 40 key houses. The Village's 2007 map shows six more potentially eligible districts, including North and South Ridgewood Road, Riggs Place and Meeker Street.

  6. Trace the deeds

    A title search at the Essex County Register of Deeds follows the chain of owners. The Stone House nomination shows how far back a chain can go: to a sheriff's sale in 1852 and beyond.

  7. Search the papers

    Look for the South Orange Bulletin, the News-Record of Maplewood and South Orange (viewable at the Maplewood Library), the Newark papers and the New York Times archive.

  8. Meet the people

    Census records (Ancestry is free at the library) and Columbia High School yearbooks put faces to the names in your deed chain. Remember that before 1904 South Orange's records are mixed in with today's Maplewood, which shared the township.

How this site works

Facts, stories & pictures

Facts. Every dated place and event comes from one of the sources listed below, and each card links to its source. Where sources disagree, we say so. For example, the Old Stone House is dated anywhere from c. 1670 to 1680, and the railroad's arrival from 1836 to October 1837.

House records. House pages add four kinds of original material when they exist for an address: residents from the Oranges city directories (1908–1950, names through 1950 only), mentions in historic newspapers from the Library of Congress (1869–1963), the matching sheets of the 1912 and 1949 Sanborn fire-insurance maps, and Gottscho-Schleisner photographs. All are public domain or carry no known restrictions. We leave out collections whose owners reserve reproduction rights, such as Seton Hall's postcards.

Stories. The "what it was like" passages and the house stories are our own narrative synthesis of those facts. A house story is about the neighborhood and the spot, not a record of the specific building.

Pictures. The Time Machine panorama is an artistic interpretation. Buildings are arranged for storytelling, not placed where they actually stand, and the "postcard" and "Kodachrome" looks are filters. For real historic photographs, visit the library's digital archive.

Boundaries and names. Before 1904 South Orange was part of larger townships. No surveyed lines survive for them, so the Explore map draws each era's outline from today's town lines and marks it approximate. The town's name on every page follows the year you choose.

Not official. This is an unofficial fan site with no affiliation to the Village or the organizations above.
