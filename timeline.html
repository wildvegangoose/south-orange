Before 1666

Lenapehoking

A trail through the chestnut woods

Called: Lenapehoking

Before Europeans arrived, this slope below the first Watchung ridge was part of Lenapehoking, the Lenape homeland. Durand-Hedden calls the Lenape "the earliest residents of the Maplewood-South Orange area."

Park & Land

The Path to Newark Bay

📍 South Orange Avenue

Tradition calls South Orange Avenue an Indian trail that "served as the main thoroughfare." Newark surveyed it in 1705 as a road "from said Riggs to town… by a path as straight as may be." No archaeological site has been found in the Village.

1666–1739

Chestnut Hill on the Newark Mountain

A stone house before there was a town

Called: Newark Mountains

Puritan settlers founded Newark in 1666 and bought land reaching to the mountains. On September 27, 1680 the town granted Nathaniel Wheeler and Edward and Joseph Riggs land "upon the upper Chesnut hill by Raway River, near the Stone House." That stone house is the first building recorded here.

1677
House

Brown Brothers' Farm (site)

📍 Tillou Road & Ridgewood Road

Joseph and Thomas Brown cleared a farm here; Wikipedia dates it to 1677. A 1686 survey notes "a House on it, built by Joseph Brown and Thomas Brown." In 1884 the land was still in the family, owned by A. F. Tillou. Some argue this, not the Old Stone House, was the 1680 "Stone House." The house is gone.

Old Stone House

📍 201 South Orange Avenue (Block 1007 Lot 13)

First mentioned in Newark's grant of September 27, 1680, land "near the Stone House." Its standing stone wing is Bethuel Pierson's work of 1773–91, the only pre-1800 building left in the Village. Owners included the Lindsleys, John Gorham Vose and William A. Brewer Jr., who called it "Aldworth"; the Village bought it in 1953. On the National Register since 1991.

Riggs Homestead (site)

📍 South Orange Avenue & Ridgewood Road (NW corner)

Joseph Riggs's house stood at the junction of Newark's two 1705 roads. Daniel and Sarah Riggs rebuilt it in stone with a "D. S. R. 1774" datestone. The Church of the Holy Communion later received the lot "on the site of the old Riggs homestead."

1740–1805

Stone Houses and Revolution

Pierson's stone addition and a name in an advertisement

Called: Newark Mountains → South Orange

By the 1740s the neighborhood's account books list the Ball, Crowell, Pierson, Smith, Hedden, Condit, Brown, Williams, Harrison and Freeman households. Bethuel Pierson added a stone wing to his house by the Stone House Brook. The National Register dates it to 1773–91, and it still stands as the Old Stone House.

1774
House

Squier House

📍 167 North Ridgewood Road

Henry Squier's c. 1774 house had a brownstone first story. William Redmond bought the farm in 1850 and leased the house to dairyman Flood, who gave his name to Flood's Hill. It was largely rebuilt around 2021; only its two oldest rooms were kept.

Stone School on the Common

📍 South Orange Avenue at Irvington Avenue (approx.)

The village's first public building: a 20-by-30-foot stone schoolhouse with an open belfry on the "school-house common," where the roads to Newark met. Its earliest record is an account entry of June 20, 1787. The turnpike builders later found it "standing in the way."

1806–1836

The Turnpike Village

School, church and thirty dwellings

Called: South Orange → South Orange, Orange Township

In 1806 Orange Township was set off from Newark, and South Orange became its southern hamlet. The Newark and Morris Turnpike, chartered in 1811, ran through South Orange along today's South Orange Avenue. Its builders found the stone school "standing in the way."

Civic

Columbian School

📍 South Orange Avenue at Irvington Avenue (approx.)

About 75 families named their school the "Columbian School of South Orange" on August 3, 1814. A two-story wooden schoolhouse was finished on the common in 1815, and its upper floor hosted singing schools and shows. In 1818 the trustees waived firewood fees for poor children. A brick school replaced it in 1880.

1861–1890

Township and Village

Gaslight, Montrose and the 1869 charter

Called: South Orange Township → Village of South Orange

In 1861 South Orange Township was formed, taking in today's South Orange and Maplewood. Those who had "made extensive improvements" wanted to manage their own affairs, so in 1869 the Village of South Orange was chartered inside the township. L. L. Coudert was its first president.

South Orange Township Formed

📍 Township-wide

In 1861 South Orange Township was created from parts of Clinton Township and Orange. It covered today's South Orange and Maplewood, plus Vailsburg until 1894. Its committee first met on April 13, 1861.

Vose's Montrose

📍 Montrose, Raymond, Ralston, Warwick, Charlton & Irving Avenues

Between 1867 and 1874 John Gorham Vose and Henry A. Page laid out streets "to attract wealthy New York businessmen to rural South Orange." Vose assembled more than 175 acres, and his deeds allowed one house per lot "appropriate for a gentleman's country residence." Lewis P. Taylor's May 1873 map shows Vose's lots.

Montrose Park and the Civic Center

Village Hall, a library and a fire company

Called: Village of South Orange

In February 1891 the volunteer fire department organized, and the Village annexed the land east of Centre Street. Thomas S. Kingman's syndicate laid out Montrose Park there, with lots of at least 100 by 200 feet. By 1896 about forty large houses stood on its streets.

1904–1929

The Independent Village

Elevated tracks and a doubling of population

Called: Village of South Orange

Independent from 1904, the Village bought Grove Park in 1908 and dug its own wells in 1912. Tuxedo Park's first houses went up in 1911. The railroad was raised above the streets in 1914–16, and the present South Orange station opened on February 1, 1916.

1904
Event

The Village Goes Its Own Way

📍 Village-wide

On March 4, 1904 the Village separated from South Orange Township. No source states its motive; under the old arrangement it "had no control over the township tax assessor's" valuations. The remaining township renamed itself Maplewood in 1922.
