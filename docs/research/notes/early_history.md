# Early History of South Orange, NJ (Lenape times to 1860)

Scope and attribution rules used in these notes:
- "SO village" means today's Township of South Orange Village. "SO Township (1861)" means the larger South Orange Township formed in 1861. It included today's Maplewood (Jefferson Village/Hilton) and parts of Vailsburg (now Newark). Shaw's 1884 chapter "South Orange Township" (written by Daniel T. Clark) covers that larger township. Many of its families, houses and mills are in today's Maplewood, and I flag those.
- Main primary or near-primary sources:
  - William H. Shaw (comp.), *History of Essex and Hudson Counties, New Jersey*, Vol. II (1884), Chapter LXIII "South Orange Township", by Daniel T. Clark, approx. pp. 779–800. Full OCR text: https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw. Quotes below come from that OCR, and OCR errors are noted where they matter. Page numbers are read from the OCR running heads and are approximate.
  - James Hoyt, *The Mountain Society* (1860): https://archive.org/details/themountainsociet00hoyt
  - The Montrose Park Historic District National Register nomination (listed 1997): https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd. It cites Gordon's Gazetteer (1834), the Hughes map (1860), Whittemore (1896) and Pierson (1922).
- Items marked "(search-result summary; page not opened)" come from search-engine digests. Verify them before publishing.

## Lenape presence, trails, the Newark purchase, and first European settlement of the "Chestnut Hill" / Newark Mountains area

### Takeaway
South Orange land came to the Newark settlers through the 1666/67 Newark purchase and the 1678 westward extension to the mountain top. Newark grants "upon the upper Chestnut hill by Raway River, near the Stone House" date from 27 September 1680. The first documented families on the land were the Browns, Riggses, Freemans, Wheelers, Tompkinses, Tichenors and Balls, and the first recorded roads (1705) followed existing paths. That South Orange Avenue follows a Lenape trail is a widely repeated local tradition, but I found no primary source that documents it.

### Cited Findings
**Lenape and land purchases**
- "The earliest residents of the Maplewood-South Orange area were The Lenape." The Durand-Hedden archive piece cites Robert Grumet's book for the regional picture — [Durand-Hedden, "The Lenape Who Came Before Us"](https://www.durandhedden.org/post/the-lenape-who-came-before-use)
- Local lore holds that a "Chief Tuscan (or Tuskin)", "reputedly born a Mohican", lived "in the ravine along what is now Tuscan Road" (this is Maplewood) and died in 1801. The article adds that "Some historians, including Mr. Kraft, believe that the Native Americans were long gone from this area by that time." In the early 1920s James Ricalton and William Sayer explored "the swampy area near the East Branch of the Rahway River" (Maplewood Memorial Park / Tuscan School area). The article originally ran 5 Aug 2008 — [Village Green reprint, 13 Jul 2020](https://villagegreennj.com/?p=73712). This is a Maplewood tradition, not a South Orange one.
- The Village's official history says the settlers "purchased land, now known as Newark, from the Lenni Lenape Indians on July 11, 1666." It also says "In 1678, the Lenapes sold the settlers a second parcel of land running from the East Branch of the Rahway River" (to the mountain top) — [southorange.org History](https://southorange.org/342/History)
- The South Orange Public Library timeline: "July 11th, 1666: Purchase of land, which includes South Orange, was negotiated with the Hackensack branch of the Lenni Lenape tribe." — [SOPL Local History wiki, Historical Timeline](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- **Conflicting dates for the purchase.** The Newark Public Library chronology has three entries:
  - Newark was "settled in May [1666] by immigrants from Connecticut led by Robert Treat."
  - "The Hackensack tribe was compensated for exchange of lands by Captain Robert Treat on March 13" (filed under 1667).
  - Under 1678: "Newark's township boundary was extended west to the top of the Orange Mountains."
  — [Knowing Newark chronology 1666–1699](https://knowingnewark.npl.org/chronology/1666-1699/)
  Several other sources date the formal Newark Indian deed to **11 July 1667**, not 1666, and one dates the mountain extension to 13 March 1675 (search-result summary of ldsgenealogy/Paterson-history pages; pages not opened). Treat the "July 11, 1666" date used by the Village and SOPL as disputed. The deed may be July 11, 1667.
- Wikipedia: the land was "purchased from the Lenape Native Americans in 1666 by Robert Treat"; "The unsettled areas north and west of Newark were at first referred to as the uplands"; "South Orange was called the Chestnut Hills for a time." — [Wikipedia, South Orange, NJ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)

**Trails and the first roads**
- The Village says "South Orange Avenue, an Indian trail, served as the main thoroughfare," and "in 1705, road statutes required landowners to maintain the first primitive highways." — [southorange.org History](https://southorange.org/342/History)
- Durand-Hedden: "Early roads from Newark were surveyed, often along old Indian trails, two of which eventually became South Orange and Clinton Avenues." — [Durand-Hedden, History of Durand-Hedden](https://www.durandhedden.org/history-of-durand-hedden)
- Primary road records, quoted by Clark in Shaw (1884), approx. pp. 792–793 — [Shaw 1884 Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw):
  - Town meeting, 12 Dec. 1681: surveyors chosen "to lay out highways as far as the mountain, if need be." Clark notes that the 1705 surveys refer to "paths" that "had been used for so long a time" as to justify referencing them.
  - 8 Oct. 1705, road no. 1: "A road from town to the foot of the Mountain or Wheeler's, as the path now runs, as straight as the ground will allow." Clark identifies this with Main Street, Orange.
  - 8 Oct. 1705, road no. 2: "Another road from said road, south by a line of marked trees, to Joseph Riggs' house." Clark: "the Valley or Freemantown road, now the Ridgewood road."
  - 8 Oct. 1705, road no. 3: "Another road from said Riggs to town, to run by a path as straight as may be…" Clark: "This clearly is South Orange [Avenue]."
  - 1 Aug. 1705: a highway running "over Rahway River, then along by a line of marked trees till it comes to Minneesink path, at the rear of Edward Riggs' plantation." This is the only Native-named path in the local records I found.
- Wikipedia (from the 1705 road survey): a Joseph Riggs house stood at the northwest corner of today's South Orange Avenue and Ridgewood Road (paraphrase) — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)

**First grants and families (Shaw/Clark 1884, approx. pp. 780–791, unless noted)** — [Shaw 1884 Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- **27 Sept. 1680 town vote:** "Nathaniel Wheeler, Edward Riggs and Joseph Riggs, have a Grant to take up Lands upon the upper Chesnut hill by Raway River, near the Stone House; provided they exceed not above sixty Acres apiece." Clark's footnote on "the Stone House": "Probably the first house erected in what is now South Orange." Wikipedia quotes the same 1680 grant — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- **Brown family:** John Brown "came from Milford with the first settlers in 1666." A 1686 town vote let his sons Joseph and Thomas Brown take up their father's third-division land "on this side Raway River, below the Mouth of Stone House Brook". Joseph's 1686 survey (30 acres "on the mountain side down to Rahway River") carries the note: "this Land hath a House on it, built by Joseph Brown and Thomas Brown, either of them having an equal share of it." Clark (1884) says Joseph Brown's land "has never passed out of the possession of his family" and was then owned by A. F. Tillou. This is the origin of the Tillou Road association.
- Wikipedia: "In 1677 brothers Joseph and Thomas Brown began clearing land for a farm." — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- **Riggs family:** "Serjeant Edward Riggs… was at Milford in 1640." He and his son Joseph appear "among the original list of the settlers of Newark", and Edward died before 1670. A later Joseph Riggs appears in town records 1721–1744 and died 11 Sept. 1744 aged 69. Daniel Riggs (b. 29 May 1724) "died at South Orange." His farm "extended from Rahway River to the westerly side of the mountain, the dwelling-house standing upon the site now occupied by the rectory of the church of the Holy Communion." The "old stone house which Daniel Riggs added to and rebuilt in 1774" had a datestone with a double heart and "D. S. R. 1774" (Daniel and Sarah Riggs).
- **Freeman family:** Stephen Freeman, "from Milford", signed the original agreement and his will is dated 1667. His widow Hannah had 40 acres "near the mountain". Surveys went to Samuel Freeman Jr. in 1694, with deeds in 1728 and 1732. Clark: "the family of Freemans were among the first settlers of South Orange."
- **Tichenor:** "Martin Tichenor, one of the original settlers of Newark", had a survey dated 27 April 1694 "at the Mountain… bounded east by the River, west by the Mountain, north John Brown, south Thomas Brown." John Tichenor's stone house on Irvington Avenue was "erected about one hundred and thirty years ago" (c. 1750s, counting back from 1884).
- **Ball:** Edward Ball, "from Branford, 1664[?]" (OCR "1GI54"), was High Sheriff of Essex in 1693. Clark: "If Edward Ball was not personally a settler in South Orange his descendants were." A biography in the same volume says Aaron Ball "located in South Orange during the year 1741" (approx. p. 800).
- **Other early names:** Nathaniel Wheeler had a survey "at Upper Chestnut Hill, by the Stone House Brook", though Clark thinks he did not live there. Clark also lists Tompkins, Luddington, Lindsley, Ward, Baldwin, Smith, Crowell, Hedden, Condit, Pierson, Squier and Harrison.
- Daniel Riggs's account book (first entry "September ye 28th, 1744") names the local residents of the 1740s. They include Aaron Ball, Samuel Crowell, Bethuel Pierson, William Smith, Ebenezer Hedden, Samuel Condit, Job Brown, Nathaniel Williams, Timothy Ball, Amos Harrison, Samuel Freeman and others. These cover the user's list (Freeman, Ball, Tichenor, Brown, Condit, Harrison, Williams, Smith).
- Durand-Hedden: "Early families such as the Balls, Crowells, Baldwins, Beaches, Browns, Smiths, and Heddens bought large plots of land." — [Durand-Hedden, History](https://www.durandhedden.org/history-of-durand-hedden)
- Wikipedia: a 1686 survey notes "this Land hath a House on it, built by Joseph Brown and Thomas Brown." — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)

### Inferences
- The documented "trail" evidence is the 1705 wording "as the path now runs" / "by a path as straight as may be" for the road Clark identifies as South Orange Avenue, plus a "Minneesink path" near Edward Riggs's land. That is consistent with, but does not prove, the "Lenape trail" tradition. The path could also have been a settlers' cart path made after 1666.
- The "Chestnut Hill" name is documented early: the 1680 grant says "upper Chesnut hill", and a later deed calls it "at the mountain plantation, so-called, at a place commonly called and known by the name of Chestnut Hill" (OCR date garbled, Shaw p. ~785).

### Gaps
- I found no archaeological report of a Lenape camp inside SO village, and no named Lenape site there. The Tuscan story is a Maplewood tradition.
- I could not get a primary text of the Newark deed to settle 11 July 1666 vs. 11 July 1667, or the date and grantors of the mountain purchase (1675 vs. 1677/1678).
- I did not obtain Beatrice Herman, *The Trail to Upland Plantations* (1976), which is the source behind the 1677 Brown date and the Tillou-house theory.

## How the name "South Orange" arose and when it first appears

### Takeaway
"Orange" (first "Orangefield"/"Orange Dale") was a late-18th-century name for the Newark "Mountain Society" parish around today's Orange center. "South Orange" was its southern neighborhood. Sources give five different "first" appearances of "South Orange": a 1793 *Wood's Gazette* ad, a 1795 Nathan Squier wood-sale ad (tradition), 1798, an 1800 deed, and Shaw's undated tradition. The origin is undocumented.

### Cited Findings
- **Shaw/Clark 1884:** "The time and circumstances under which the name South Orange originated will, probably, never be known, and we are obliged to fall back on a tradition, that Mr. Nathan Squier first used the name in an advertisement offering wood for sale at vendue." (approx. p. 780; no year given) — [Shaw 1884 Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- **Wikipedia** dates the Squier tradition to **1795**. It also says an **1800** deed refers to "the Township of Newark, in the Parish of Orange, at a place called South Orange", which marked the end of the name Chestnut Hills — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- **Village of South Orange:** "The name South Orange first appeared in print in a newspaper ad in 1793 in 'Wood's Gazette.'" — [southorange.org History](https://southorange.org/342/History)
- **SOPL:** "1798: First record of the village name of South Orange, but not yet separate from Newark as a Township." It also has "1782: Town name of 'Orange Dale' informally adopted." — [SOPL Historical Timeline](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- **West Orange historian Joe Fagan** (Patch), each line quoted:
  - "The name Orangefield was first used and adopted by the settlers in 1780 for an area of land west of Newark."
  - "in 1782 the name of Orangedale was first used to designate the place of a church meeting."
  - "by 1796, the 'dale' suffix was dropped."
  - The prefix "is reported to have been derived from… the House of Orange."
  - "On November 27, 1806, Orange was established as a separate township."
  — [Patch, "Why There's No North Orange"](https://patch.com/new-jersey/westorange/why-there-no-north-orange-historian-explains-nj-oddity)
- **Hoyt (1860), primary church records:** "The Presbytery of New York, as its records inform us, met at Orange Dale, in October, 1785." His footnote adds "Not in 1786 or 1787, as given by Dr. Stearns." Hoyt says of the village name: "By whom, or from what circumstance the name was first bestowed, we have no means of ascertaining." He also quotes an 18th-century ad for lots "pleasantly situated in Orange Dale, on the main road, opposite the meeting-house, and adjoining the Academy". This shows "Orange Dale" meant the Orange meeting-house village (Main Street, Orange), not South Orange — [Hoyt, The Mountain Society (1860)](https://archive.org/details/themountainsociet00hoyt)
- **Conflict:** Britannica describes South Orange as "originally the Orange Dale section of Orange township" (search-result summary; page not opened) — [Britannica, South Orange Village](https://britannica.com/place/South-Orange-Village). This conflicts with Hoyt's evidence that "Orange Dale" was the Orange meeting-house village. Treat Britannica's wording as a conflation.
- Shaw also records a 1796 deed for land beginning at "the Road leading from South Orange to Newark" (Jonathan Ward to Jabez and Isaac Pierson, dated 29 March 1796; OCR "179ti"). That would be an early documentary use of "South Orange", if the OCR year is right (approx. p. 785) — [Shaw 1884 Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)

### Inferences
- "South Orange" was probably an informal directional name for the southern district of Orange parish in the 1790s. The 1796 deed wording "Road leading from South Orange to Newark" (if OCR is right) and the 1800 deed's "Parish of Orange, at a place called South Orange" both fit that reading.
- "Wood's Gazette" is presumably John Woods's *Newark Gazette*, but I did not verify this or locate the 1793 ad.

### Gaps
- I did not find the 1793 *Wood's Gazette* ad, the 1795 Squier ad, the source behind the SOPL's 1798 "first record", or the full 1800 deed. Each source asserts its date without citing the document.

## Colonial and Revolutionary-era buildings and events in what is now South Orange

### Takeaway
The only widely recognized colonial building in SO village is the Old Stone House ("Stone House by the Stone House Brook"), 219 South Orange Ave. Its claimed pre-1680 date is disputed, and a fair reading of the evidence is c. 1767–1774, with Bethuel Pierson's stone work. Revolutionary connections are traditions rather than documented events: an undated skirmish "Battle of Chestnut Hill" after Springfield (June 1780), local militiamen, and a Ball killed at Springfield. The nearby 1743 Timothy Ball House and its Washington-cousin legend are in Maplewood.

### Cited Findings
**Old Stone House / Stone House by the Stone House Brook (SO village)**
- Address 219 South Orange Avenue; coordinates 40.74500, −74.25361. NRHP #87001333, listed 22 Nov 1991; NJRHP #1364, 4 Oct 1991. "Built: pre-1680."
- Earliest reference: Newark minutes of 27 Sept 1680.
- Dr. Bethuel Pierson, "a Revolutionary War patriot and mill operator", owned it 1773–1791 and "enlarged it in a similar style using rubble stone."
- W. A. Brewer Jr. bought it in 1866 and named it "Aldworth". The Village bought it in 1953 and used it as school offices until 1983. It was placed on Preservation NJ's Ten Most Endangered list in 2020.
— [Wikipedia, Stone House by the Stone House Brook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone_House_by_the_Stone_House_Brook)
- SOHPS: the house "is believed to be the oldest standing building in New Jersey"; "The earliest reference to the Old Stone House dates to September 27, 1680". The society says historians estimate Dutch settlers built it between 1666 and 1680, as a 1½-story house on 75 acres. Pierson bought it in 1773, and Brewer moved in in 1867. It "sits nearly out of sight behind the South Orange police station between Grove Road and South Orange Avenue." — [SOHPS, Old Stone House](https://sohps.org/old-stone-house/)
- **Conflict on Brewer's purchase:** Wikipedia says 1866, with occupancy 1867–1916. SOHPS says he moved in 1867. The Montrose nomination says he "bought the Old Stone House in 1867" — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
- **Dating dispute** (blog by "Joe B.", 2009):
  - The 1680 grant only authorized taking up land "near the Stone House". That stone house was probably the Brown brothers' house near Tillou Road, which Beatrice Herman (1976) dated to 1677.
  - The oldest documented house on the present site appears in a 1767 survey.
  - The stone portion looks like Pierson's c. 1773–74 work, and archaeological finds there date no earlier than c. 1750.
  - The author calls the evidence "too sketchy" to settle the question.
  — [War of Yesterday blog, "Stone House" (28 Jun 2009)](https://warofyesterday.blogspot.com/2009/06/stone-house.html)
- **Primary support for the Pierson stone addition** (Shaw/Clark 1884):
  - On 11 Jan 1773 "Bethuel Pierson… gave a mortgage upon one hundred acres, whereon he now lives at the mountain plantation by a certain brook called Stone House Brook."
  - "Deacon Bethuel Pierson had a stone addition added to his dwelling-house, which he caused to be dedicated by religious ceremonies." He "died in 1791, aged 90 years."
  - Cyrus Pierson sold the 116 acres to Nathaniel Condit on 11 May 1793. The house was later occupied by "William A. Brewer, president of the Washington Life Insurance Company".
  (approx. p. 783) — [Shaw 1884 Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Wikipedia (South Orange) adds: the Stone House is "First mentioned in a document in 1680." By 1756 the property was owned by Samuel Pierson, and a 1767 survey mentions "Pierson's house." — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- **Do not confuse it with other Pierson houses.** Joseph Pierson's "stone house" stood at the corner of Jefferson Avenue and the road to Pierson's Mills, in today's Maplewood (Shaw p. ~783). A 2009 blog comment points to the "Old Stone House at 22 Jefferson Avenue in Maplewood", which Bethuel Pierson also owned — [War of Yesterday blog](https://warofyesterday.blogspot.com/2009/06/stone-house.html)

**Other 18th-century houses mentioned (survival and location uncertain unless stated)**
- SOPL: "1774: Squire Family homestead built"; "Circa 1760: The Jeptha Baldwin homestead built. Still standing today." No addresses given — [SOPL Historical Timeline](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- Shaw/Clark (approx. pp. 785, 790):
  - "Henry Squier in 1774 resided in the stone house — now the farm-house — on the Redmond estate." The Redmond estate was near Ridgewood Road.
  - "Jotham Quinby… resided in a stone house, built in 1774, on the Smith property, on Scotland Street, South Orange. He demolished the old house about fifty years ago [c. 1830s]."
  — [Shaw 1884 Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Montrose nomination: "The oldest structure in the district is the Old Stone House, followed by the Abel Ward house, at 497 South Orange Avenue, and the Benjamin Baldwin house at 311 Centre Street (also known as the House with the White Chimneys)." The Baldwin ("Bishop Baldwin") house "was believed to be constructed c. 1806". These were "some of the few houses present when John Graham Vose first moved to the area in 1859." — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
- Shaw: Abel Ward's will (1792) left land and buildings to sons Aaron and Jonathan. Jonathan Ward sold c. 69 acres on "the Road leading from South Orange to Newark" to Jabez and Isaac Pierson on 29 March 1796. That land passed through Lindsley, Harrison, Amos Freeman and Thomas Lowndes, and "is now Seton Hall College grounds and farm" (approx. p. 785) — [Shaw 1884 Vol. II](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- **Timothy Ball House (MAPLEWOOD, not SO village):** Shaw: "The house built by Timothy (possibly by his father, Thomas) is still standing… on Ridgewood road. A stone in the chimney has the initial letters 'T. E. B., 1743,' in another chimney the figures 1772" (approx. p. 783) — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw). Durand-Hedden gives its address as 425 Ridgewood Road and says Timothy was a grandson of Edward Ball, who came to Newark in 1667 (search-result summary; page not opened) — [Durand-Hedden, Timothy Ball House 1743](https://www.durandhedden.org/post/timothy-ball-house-1743)

**Revolutionary War**
- SOPL: "At the time of the Revolutionary War South Orange included a few farmhouses, a stone school house," plus a blacksmith shop, gristmill, general store and tavern — [SOPL Historical Timeline](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- Patch (Jared Kofsky, 2 July 2009), citing Pierson's *History of the Oranges*, Herman (1976) and D. C. Munn's *Battles and Skirmishes*:
  - Washington's "third cousin, Timothy Ball, had built a house on Ridgewood Road in Maplewood", where Washington's men reportedly slept.
  - On 22 Nov 1776 Washington marched his soldiers along present-day South Orange Avenue to Newark.
  - An undated "Battle of Chestnut Hill", probably a skirmish after the Battle of Springfield (23 June 1780): a Ward family member heard Redcoats near Center Street and raised the alarm. "Chestnut Hill men", minutemen of the Essex Militia, fired on them in today's downtown, the Redcoats were repelled, and no one was seriously hurt.
  — [Patch, "American Revolution Came to South Orange"](https://patch.com/new-jersey/southorange/american-revolution-came-to-south-orange)
- Shaw/Clark (approx. p. 787): Samuel Ball "built and resided in a house which formerly stood upon the site of the present residence of Mr. Samuel P. Brown. Mr. Brown has a tradition that the Samuel Ball killed in battle at Springfield was a former owner." — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Shaw (Congar record, approx. p. 789): "…Esther, w[ife] of Moses Smith, killed in Rev. war, father of Moses, late sheriff of Essex." — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Shaw biography (approx. p. 800): Joseph Ball, son of Aaron Ball, who settled in South Orange in 1741, "served in the English army and assisted in the capture of Martinique. He was afterward a soldier in the war of the Revolution, and assisted in the capture of Stony Point under Gen. Anthony Wayne." — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Durand-Hedden runs Battle of Springfield history hikes, described as "right on the doorstep of Maplewood/South Orange" (search-result summary; page not opened) — [Village Green](https://villagegreennj.com/?p=11483)

### Inferences
- The Patch claim that Washington marched "along present-day South Orange Avenue to Newark" on 22 Nov 1776 is geographically doubtful. Washington's November 1776 retreat came south from Hackensack and Acquackanonk into Newark, not east from the mountains. I found no corroboration, so treat it as legend.
- The "Battle of Chestnut Hill" rests on 20th-century local histories (Pierson 1922, Herman 1976). I found no contemporary record of it. Present it as tradition.
- The SOPL entry "1774 Squire Family homestead" probably corresponds to Clark's "Henry Squier in 1774 resided in the stone house… on the Redmond estate." The 1774 date may be confused with the Daniel Riggs datestone "D. S. R. 1774" or the 1774 Quinby/Smith stone house. This is unverified.

### Gaps
- I found no muster roll of South Orange-area soldiers. A future search could use NJ Adjutant General records (Stryker 1872) for Ball, Freeman, Tichenor and Ward names.
- I found no source locating the "Jeptha Baldwin homestead (c. 1760), still standing" at an address. One genealogy says the Jeptha Baldwin farm later fell within Montrose Park (search-result summary). The 311 Centre Street "Benjamin/Bishop Baldwin" house (c. 1806) may or may not be it.
- I could not geocode 497 South Orange Avenue (Abel Ward house) reliably, and found no construction date for it.

## Civil organization (1806 Orange Township onward), mills, roads/turnpikes, early churches and schools

### Takeaway
The area belonged to Newark Township until Orange Township was set off in 1806–07. Part of it went to Clinton Township in 1834, and South Orange Township was formed in 1861 (sources give 26 Jan or 1 Apr). The village grew around a stone schoolhouse on the "school-house common" near today's South Orange Ave and Irvington Ave/Academy St. That school was reorganized as the Columbian School in 1814, rebuilt in wood in 1815, and the Presbyterian congregation (organized 13 June 1831) worshipped in it until about 1842. The Newark and Morristown Turnpike (chartered 1811) became South Orange Avenue. The 18th-century mills on the Rahway (Pierson's, Arents–Dod) were mostly in today's Maplewood.

### Cited Findings
**Civil boundaries**
- Shaw/Clark: the settlers' descendants "remained inhabitants of the town of Newark until 1806-7, at which time a division of Newark was made, and Orange ward was constituted." The division line between the Bloomfield, Newark and Orange wards was agreed "the ninth day of May, 1807" at the house of Samuel Munn in Orange (approx. pp. 779–780) — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Wikipedia: "Most of modern South Orange became part of Orange Township in 1806", then "part of Clinton Township in 1834", then "part of South Orange Township in 1861". "South Orange Village dates back to May 4, 1869, when it was formed within South Orange Township." — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- Fagan: "On November 27, 1806, Orange was established as a separate township"; "South Orange was organized on January 26, 1861." — [Patch](https://patch.com/new-jersey/westorange/why-there-no-north-orange-historian-explains-nj-oddity)
- Durand-Hedden: "The Township of South Orange was formed from Clinton Township and Orange Town" on **April 1, 1861**. The Village was formed within the Township on **May 4, 1869**. Part of Millburn Township ("known as Jefferson Village until 1860, when a railroad station was built") was added in 1863. The Village separated after a referendum of 2 March 1904 — [Durand-Hedden, "The Separation of Maplewood and South Orange"](https://www.durandhedden.org/post/the-separation-of-maplewood-and-south-orange)
- **Conflict:** Montrose nomination: "On March 25, 1869, an 'Act to Incorporate the Village of South Orange…' was placed upon the state books." SOPL: "1869 (March 25) Village charter obtained." 4 May 1869 is presumably the date of organization. The nomination also says South Orange Township "was formed in 1861 from Clinton Township and Orange Town" (citing Snyder 1969) — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd); [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- SOPL's entry "1806: South Orange separates from Newark" is loose wording: what separated was Orange Township as a whole — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)

**Roads and turnpikes**
- For the 1705 roads (Ridgewood Road = "Valley or Freemantown road"; South Orange Avenue), see the first section.
- Shaw: "In 1811 the Newark and Morristown Turnpike Road Company was chartered. This company, in the construction of their road, found the school-house standing in the way of their improvement" (approx. p. 793). Seton Hall sits "near South Orange Avenue, formerly the turnpike road" (approx. p. 798) — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Gordon's *Gazetteer of New Jersey* (1834), quoted in the Montrose nomination: South Orange "lies on the turnpike from Newark to Morristown, 5 miles west of the first; it contains about 30 dwellings, a tavern and store; a paper mill and Presbyterian church; the lands around it are rich and well farmed". The nomination adds: "settlement was concentrated around South Orange Avenue (the turnpike), Prospect Street and Irvington Avenue." On the 1860 Hughes map, South Orange Avenue was "then known as the Newark Turnpike", and "South Orange Avenue, Scotland Road, Montrose Avenue and Centre Street were the only existing roads" in the Montrose area — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd). Wikipedia dates the Gordon description "c. 1830" — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- Scotland Road: an early deed records Isaac Ogden conveying "12 acres and 13 rods of land on Scotland Road… formerly the place of Jeptha Baldwin, deceased" to Ezra Gildersleeve in 1798 (Shaw p. ~789). Shaw also calls it "Scotland Street", as in the Quinby stone house of 1774 — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Matters Magazine says a new toll road "(now South Orange Avenue) going into Newark threatened" the old school's location, so a wooden two-story school was built nearby for 1815 (search-result summary; page not opened) — [Matters Magazine, "From School House to High School"](https://www.mattersmagazine.com/post/from-school-house-to-high-school-by-adrianna-donat)
- Durand-Hedden mentions the "Newark-Springfield Turnpike… built in 1806." That is Springfield Avenue, serving Maplewood and Irvington, not South Orange — [Durand-Hedden, History](https://www.durandhedden.org/history-of-durand-hedden)

**Mills (most are in today's Maplewood)**
- Shaw/Clark, "Early Mills" (approx. pp. 792–793):
  - Surveys of 1718 refer to "the saw-mill of Dr. Arents and Daniel Dod" on the Rahway River near the Newark–Elizabethtown line. It "has long since been destroyed, leaving the ruins of the wheel-pit, (called by the boys of the neighborhood 'the tumbling-place')".
  - "Joseph Pierson's two mills, a grist and saw-mill, on the same stream (Rahway River) were well-known institutions" when he sold half of them. The deed is dated 19 Jan 1739/40: Thomas and Aaron Ball "bought of Joseph Pierson the equal one-half part of a grist mill and a saw mill on the east branch of Rahway River, near the dwelling-house of Samuel Crowell." These became Pierson's Mills in Jefferson Village, now Maplewood.
  - "The older inhabitants of South Orange speak of a saw-mill upon Rahway River, situated near the Montrose station."
  — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Caleb Johnson (from 1776) lived on Stone House Brook and "had a brewery and distilled essences and extracts of various kinds" (Shaw p. ~788) — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- The Gordon (1834) "paper mill" is unlocated (see above).

**Schools: the stone schoolhouse and the Columbian School (SO village)**
All from Shaw/Clark, "Educational", approx. pp. 793–795 — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw):
- "The earliest known record referring to a school-house in South Orange is found in the account book of Joseph Brown, under date of June 20, 1787". The entries are for mason's repairs.
- "The building, of stone, was probably built long anterior to the above date." Mrs. Rhoda Baldwin, "born in South Orange in the year 1784", attended it, and believed her mother (born 1757; OCR "17-')7") had too.
- Location and form: it "stood in front of the present school-house, on a point of land near the intersection of the roads to Newark, now known as South Orange and Irvington Avenues; the building was one story in height, about twenty by thirty feet, facing the east, having a vestibule which was surmounted by an open belfry, in which the bell was hung."
- Lewis Tichenor's account book shows "he taught a school in South Orange in the years 1791, '92, '93, '94, '95, '97 and '98."
- **1814 reorganization:**
  - A special meeting of "the proprietors and associates of the school in South Orange", held on a Wednesday evening in 1814, agreed to incorporate. The OCR date reads "Jui> 22, 1814". July 22, 1814 was a Friday; June 22 and July 20, 1814 were Wednesdays, so the exact date is uncertain.
  - "On August 3d another meeting was held, at which time the trustees were elected and the name 'Columbian School of South Orange' was adopted."
  - The list of proprietors names about 75 heads of families: Squier, Brown, Pierson, Baldwin, Ball, Tichenor, Freeman, Crowell, Hedden, Condit, Ward, Taylor, Kilburn, Quinby, Tillou and others.
- On "Saturday evening, Dec. 31, 1814" the trustees resolved to build a new school-house "of wood, forty-five feet in length by thirty feet in breadth", two stories ("stories high" in the OCR). "The work… was completed in 1815."
- On 26 Oct 1815 tuition was set: "$1.75 per quarter for spelling, reading and writing", with extra charges for arithmetic and grammar/geography.
- By a deed dated 1 Jan 1816 (recorded 12 May 1828), Aaron Brown and wife conveyed the 0.76-acre lot on "the school-house common" for $31. The house and lot cost "$1,716.18".
- 27 Jan 1818: firewood fees were waived for children unable to pay, "the first step toward making common-school education free to all".
- The upper floor was rented "for singing-schools, shows of different kinds, between 1816 and 1827". From 1828 it was used for religious meetings.
- A new brick school was authorized in 1879 and built c. 1880.
- Matters Magazine: "On Aug. 3, 1814, the school was named Columbian School of South Orange by seven trustees". The stone schoolhouse stood "on a grassy area known as the Common, located close to the present intersection of South Orange Avenue and Academy Street", a site reportedly now PNC Bank (search-result summary; page not opened) — [Matters Magazine](https://www.mattersmagazine.com/post/from-school-house-to-high-school-by-adrianna-donat)
- SOPL: "Circa 1814-1815: Beginning of a formal school system with The Columbian School. Original wooden building was replaced in 1880." — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- Columbia High School (now at 17 Parker Ave, Maplewood) traces its establishment to 1814. Wikipedia lists both 1814 and 1885 (search-result summary) — [Wikipedia, Columbia High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_High_School_(New_Jersey))

**Churches**
- In Shaw/Clark's account, before 1831 South Orange people attended the Second Church of Newark, later the First Presbyterian Church of Orange, "organized about 1720". North Farms (Hilton) attended Elizabethtown and Connecticut Farms, and Vauxhall/Jefferson Village attended Springfield (approx. p. 798) — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- At a meeting of the Columbian School Association "at the house of B. Harrison" in January 1828 (OCR date garbled), it was resolved to warm and light the school room "for the purpose of religious worship". "The room was used for purposes of religious worship until 1842. The Presbyterian Church of South Orange was organized June 13 [OCR 'Vi'], 1831, the congregation meeting in the school-house until their church was erected, in 1842." (approx. p. 799) — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Hoyt (1860): "In May, twenty-nine members were dismissed, who on the 13th of June were constituted as a Presbyterian church at South Orange. Elder Samuel Freeman was one of the number… The first minister was Rev. Cyrus Gildersleeve… [who] preached there as stated supply till the first of May, 1833." In 1860 the "South Orange Presbyterian Church, organized in 1831" had 157 communicants, about 100 families, and pastor Rev. Daniel G. Sprague — [Hoyt 1860](https://archive.org/details/themountainsociet00hoyt)
- **Conflict on dates:** SOPL says "1831-1832: The first village church was organized: First Presbyterian Church of South Orange" and "1843: The congregation… occupied a wooden church structure on the site". Shaw says the church was erected in 1842. On 19 April 1843 the church trustees also leased the school's upper room for 15 years — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline); [Shaw](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Today's congregation is First Presbyterian & Trinity Church, 111 Irvington Avenue. First Presbyterian (1831) and Trinity (1892) merged in 1935, and the present brownstone church dates to 1880/81 on the same triangular lot (search-result summaries; pages not opened) — [churcheslist.com](https://churcheslist.com/first-presbyterian-and-trinity-church/); [The Four Oranges](https://thefouroranges.com/firstpresbyteriansouthorange)
- Hoyt (1860): "Methodist Episcopal Church, South Orange. Formed in 1850… a small house of worship." And: "A Protestant Episcopal Society was formed, in October, 1859, at South Orange… Its religious services are held in the Methodist Church." — [Hoyt 1860](https://archive.org/details/themountainsociet00hoyt)
- Shaw: the school room was granted to "the Episcopal clergyman of Millburn" for Saturday services. On 4 Oct 1858 (OCR "18.58") it was leased to the Church of the Holy Communion, and again in 1859. The 1858 date conflicts with Hoyt's October 1859 formation date and may be an OCR or source error — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- Township-only churches (today's Maplewood, not SO village): the Baptist Church of Jefferson Village (lot deeded 6 Oct 1811 by Caleb Durand, later Methodist, c. 1823) and the Universalist church at North Farms/Hilton (1828) — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)

**Other services**
- SOPL: "1841: First post office opened in John D. Freeman's general store." — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- SOPL: "1860: Street lamps change over from burning sperm whale oil to a gas service." This is unverified elsewhere — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)

### Inferences
- The stone school, the "school-house common" and the later Columbian School and Presbyterian church cluster form the original civic center of SO village. It lies around today's South Orange Avenue between Scotland Road and Irvington Avenue/Academy Street. Shaw names South Orange and Irvington Avenues; Matters Magazine names Academy Street. Both describe the same village-center node.
- Shaw's "Montrose station" for the remembered saw mill (1884 usage) probably means a Montrose-area stop, perhaps today's Mountain Station area. This is unverified.

### Gaps
- I found no source on early Valley Road, beyond Clark's naming of Ridgewood Road as "the Valley or Freemantown road".
- The location and owner of the 1834 "paper mill" are not found.
- The tavern (Gordon 1834) is unnamed and unlocated.
- I could not resolve the exact 1814 meeting date or the 1858/1859 Holy Communion lease date (OCR issues). Checking the printed page images of Shaw (archive.org) would resolve them.

## The Morris & Essex Railroad arrival (1836–1838) and the first South Orange stop

### Takeaway
The Morris & Essex Railroad was incorporated on 29 Jan 1835 and opened from Newark to Orange on 19 Nov 1836. It reached South Orange in September 1837: a preliminary trip on 17 Sept and regular service on 28 Sept, though one source says 2 Oct. It reached Morristown on 1 Jan 1838. Local histories claiming an 1836 horse-drawn single track "between the Village and Orange" are not corroborated by railroad histories. I found no description of the first South Orange depot. Today's station (17 Sloan St) dates from 1916.

### Cited Findings
- M&E "incorporated January 29, 1835"; "The first section, from Newark west to Orange, opened on November 19, 1836"; "On January 1, 1838, the M&E extended their route to Morristown." Horse power is mentioned only for 1843, for street running in Newark — [Wikipedia, Morris and Essex Railroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morris_and_Essex_Railroad)
- South Orange station infobox: opened "September 17, 1837 (preliminary trip)" and "September 28, 1837 (regular service)". It cites "Morris and Essex is Seventy-Nine Years Old," *The Madison Eagle*, 16 June 1916, p. 10, and H. T. Walker, *Railroad Gazette* vol. 34 (1902), p. 409. The current building, at 17 Sloan Street (40.74583, −74.26083), was built by the Lackawanna in 1916 (designer Frank J. Nies), when the grade was raised — [Wikipedia, South Orange station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange_station)
- Orange station opened 19 Nov 1836 and "served as the western terminus of the line until September 28, 1837" (search-result summary) — [Wikipedia, Orange station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orange_station_(NJ_Transit))
- **Conflict:** a Patch article dates the start of South Orange rail service to 2 Oct 1837 (search-result summary; page not opened) — [Patch, Edison/first electric train](https://patch.com/new-jersey/southorange/thomas-edison-drove-first-electric-train-to-so)
- Village history: "in 1836, the Morris and Essex Railroad developed a single track between the Village and Orange". "The advent of the railroad established South Orange as a suburb of Newark and a summer resort." — [southorange.org](https://southorange.org/342/History)
- SOPL: "1836: The Morris and Essex Railroad begin operating a horse-drawn car on a single track between S.O. Village and Orange." "1837: Morris & Essex train line extended west to Madison, east to Newark." This conflicts with the Newark→Orange opening of 1836 — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- Wikipedia (South Orange): "The Morris and Essex Railroad opened in 1837 between Newark and Morristown." Later items, outside scope but useful for context: "Horsecar service from Newark started in 1865, running via South Orange Avenue to the station," and in 1869 the M&E became part of the DL&W main line — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- Montrose nomination: "The establishment of the Morris and Essex Railroad through South Orange in 1836 encouraged wealthy New York City and Newark businessmen to spend the summer months in the village." It also says the 1868 extension to Hoboken gave a direct NYC connection, and "By 1869… fifteen trains were running daily each way… serving a population of approximately 1,200" — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
- Jefferson Village (Maplewood) got its own station in 1860, named Maplewood — [Durand-Hedden](https://www.durandhedden.org/post/the-separation-of-maplewood-and-south-orange)

### Inferences
- The line went Newark → Orange (1836) → South Orange and west (1837), so South Orange lay west of the 1836 terminus. The local "1836 single track between the Village and Orange" may preserve memory of construction or trial running west of Orange in 1836–37, but nothing I found corroborates it. Present 1836 as "railroad reaches Orange" and 1837 as "railroad reaches South Orange".
- The 17 Sept 1837 "preliminary trip" date fell on a Sunday and the 28 Sept regular-service date on a Thursday. A Sunday trial trip is unusual, so verify against the 1916 *Madison Eagle* article.

### Gaps
- I found no description, builder or exact location of the first South Orange depot (1837), nor the opening date of the Mountain Station stop (its NRHP building is 1916).
- I did not open the 1916 *Madison Eagle* or 1902 *Railroad Gazette* sources.

## 1830s–1850s resort, estates, summer residents and the earliest suburban development

### Takeaway
From the late 1830s the railroad and the "healthful" mountain air made South Orange a summer resort. Its centerpiece was the Mountain House water-cure hotel on Ridgewood Road, dated c. 1830 or 1847. Around 1850, settlement clustered between the railroad, Prospect Street, South Orange Avenue and Fourth Street. Estate-building began in the late 1850s and 1860: the Elphinstone marble villa bought for Seton Hall in April 1860, and John Gorham Vose's arrival in 1858–1860 as the seed of Montrose. Planned suburban development (Montrose) began only after 1860, in 1867–1874.

### Cited Findings
- **1860 settlement pattern** (Hughes 1860 map, via the Montrose nomination):
  - "the most densely settled area of the Village of South Orange was located southwest of Montrose… from the railroad to Prospect Street, and from South Orange Avenue to Fourth Street."
  - In the Montrose area, other than houses on South Orange Avenue there "were only five homes": the Old Stone House, the Baldwin house at 311 Centre Street, D. J. Sprague's house on Scotland Road, H. M. Graham's at the northeast corner of Scotland Road and Montrose Avenue, and Dorothea Freeman's west of Scotland Road.
  - The 1860 landholders included Benjamin E. Baldwin, Ebenezer Deas, Samuel P. Smith, Aaron Bishop Baldwin, Thomas D. Kilburn, G. H. Wheeler, D. H. Condit, Ira T. Freeman, Job Tillou, Caleb Smith and others.
  — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
- **Resort reputation:** "In the mid-nineteenth century, the 'Oranges' enjoyed a reputation for the 'healthfulness of the locality'… The mountain air was considered to have 'life-giving' currents" (citing Shaw 1884: 717) — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
- **Mountain House water cure:** "a fashionable water-cure hotel and spa. Located on the west side of Ridgewood Road… the hotel was owned in 1850 by Mr. Lord of Lord and Taylor's… Built around 1830, the hotel accommodated 150 guests and was supervised by two physicians. The large, wood-framed hotel burnt down on August 23, 1890." Mountain Station and Mountain House Road are its "sole vestiges" — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
- SOPL agrees: "1830: Mountain House Spa built… could accommodate up to 150 guests", "burned to the ground in 1890" — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
- **Conflict:** Wikipedia says "A country resort called the Orange Mountain House was established in 1847 on Ridgewood Road", in an area now in West Orange — [Wikipedia, South Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)
- **Primary evidence that the water cure was under way by 1853:** *Report of two hundred and four interesting cases, selected from among those treated by Doctor E. Fellerer, resident physician of the Orange Mountain Hydropathic Institution, South Orange, New Jersey*, dated 1853; corporate author "Orange Mountain Water-Cure Establishment" — [archive.org / NLM record](https://archive.org/details/63921090R.nlm.nih.gov)
- **Seton Hall and the Elphinstone villa:**
  - Seton Hall was "founded as a seminary by Bishop James Roosevelt Bayley in 1856 in Madison". "In April of 1860, Bayley bought approximately 60 acres in South Orange, on either side of South Orange Avenue, from the Elphinstone family. The property included a marble villa, farmhouse, and stables on the south side… The first college building was ready to accept students in September of 1860." The Kelly estate east of campus was bought in 1901 — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
  - SOPL: "1860 (April 2): 60 acres purchased for the building of Seton Hall University"; "1860 (September): First classes" — [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
  - Shaw: Seton Hall "is located on what was formerly known as Chestnut Ridge… founded in 1856, at Madison… During the summer vacation of 1860 it was removed to its present location at South Orange." It was incorporated in the 1861 legislative session. Shaw's land chain says the former Abel and Jonathan Ward farm (sold 1796) "is now Seton Hall College grounds and farm" — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw)
- **John Gorham Vose (the start of Montrose):**
  - Village: "he purchased a home on Scotland Road in 1858", later buying large plots (from 1862) and christening the area Montrose — [southorange.org](https://southorange.org/342/History)
  - Montrose nomination: Vose "first moved to the area in 1859". Elsewhere it says that "Around 1860, a young New York lawyer, John Gorham Vose, began spending his summers in Orange Valley, in a cottage on Scotland Street, north of Tremont Avenue in Orange (Hughes 1860)", and that he "began buying land in the Montrose area in the early 1860s". The streets were "originally developed by John Gorham Vose and Henry A. Page between 1867 and 1874". George B. Turrell came "to South Orange in 1864" and bought the D. J. Sprague property on Scotland Street — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
  - **Conflict:** 1858 (Village), 1859 and c. 1860 (nomination). Even the nomination is internally inconsistent, and its c. 1860 cottage was in the City of Orange.
- **Montrose Avenue's name** predates Vose. It "appears on early maps of South Orange, including the Hughes map of 1860"; the origin is unknown, possibly the Scottish town — [Montrose NRHP nomination](https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/2b0c9ff9-6014-40a7-b3b7-c1ab7eede3dd)
- **Earlier residents and estates named by Shaw/Clark (1884)** — [Shaw 1884](https://archive.org/details/historyofessexh02shaw):
  - The Redmond estate on Ridgewood Road.
  - Mr. Eugene Kelly's lands on South Orange Avenue, next to the Kilburn homestead. The Kilburns, Jabez D. and Thomas, "lived and died at their home on South Orange Avenue".
  - Clark Hedden's house of 1815 "on the northerly side of South Orange Avenue… At that time… there were but five or six dwellings between it and High Street, in Newark."
  - Moses Taylor (b. 1767) "had a store on South Orange Avenue".
- **Pre-1870 context dates:**
  - The library association first met on 14 Nov 1864 at the house of tea merchant William J. Beebe — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey); [SOPL](https://localhistory.sopl.org/wiki/Historical_Timeline)
  - The 1865 horsecar line to the station — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Orange,_New_Jersey)

### Inferences
- "Earliest suburban development" in the strict sense of planned subdivision for commuters is post-1860 (Montrose, 1867–74). The 1837–1860 phase was a railroad village plus summer boarding and resort use (Mountain House), with a few gentlemen's estates (the Elphinstone villa, Vose, Graham, Sprague).
- The Mountain House stood on the west side of Ridgewood Road near the later Mountain House Road. Wikipedia places that area in today's West Orange, so attribute the hotel to "the South Orange area (now West Orange)". Its "South Orange" postal identity in 1853 is documented by the Fellerer report.

### Gaps
- I found no detailed sources on the 1830s–1850s estates or summer residents of SO village, such as boarding houses or named summer families before Vose. Whittemore's *Founders and Builders of the Oranges* (1896) and Pierson's *History of the Oranges* (1922) are the likely sources but were not read.
- Who the "Elphinstone family" were and the villa's construction date are not found.
- The Mountain House founding date (c. 1830 vs. 1847) and its exact site and municipality are unresolved.
